# 4. Skill Mission

The mission of this skill is to move the user from:

> “We understand our business and have many assumptions about the market.”

to:

> “We now have an evidence-based view of the market, customer needs, demand patterns, competitive landscape, pricing context, discovery and purchase channels, market opportunities, risks, and the strategic questions that Phase 3 must decide.”

Phase 2 is not a generic web-search exercise.

It is a structured evidence-building process.

It should consume:

- Business context

- Competitor clues

# 7. Required Deliverable Outcomes

The final report must allow the user to understand, in practical terms:

1. What market they are actually entering.
2. How the market is structured.
3. What major customer groups exist.
4. What customers appear to care about.
5. What problems, desires, anxieties, and purchase triggers appear repeatedly.
6. Who the direct and indirect competitors are.
7. What alternatives customers use instead of buying from the business.
8. How competitors differentiate.
9. What price levels and offer structures exist.
10. Which channels influence discovery, evaluation, and purchase.
11. What evidence supports the founder's assumptions.
12. Which assumptions were weakened or rejected.
13. What opportunities appear under-served.
14. What risks could invalidate the business direction.
15. What still remains unknown.
16. What strategic decisions should be made in Phase 3.

# 10. Research Is Not Strategy

The skill must distinguish between:

```text
WHAT THE EVIDENCE SAYS
vs.
WHAT THE BUSINESS SHOULD DECIDE
```

Example:

Research finding:

> Several high-rated competitors emphasize convenience and fast delivery.

This does not automatically mean:

> The brand should position itself around speed.

That is a strategic decision for a later phase.

# 12. Beginner-Friendly Teaching Rule

The user must not need prior market-research knowledge.

Before introducing a technical concept, explain:

```text
What is it?
↓
Why does it matter?
↓
Simple example
↓
How we will use it here
```

Keep explanations practical and short.

Do not turn Phase 2 into an academic research course.

---

# 13. Example of Beginner Translation

Do not say only:

> We need to estimate TAM, SAM, and SOM.

Instead say:

> We want to understand three levels:
>
> 1. How large the whole relevant market could be.
> 2. Which part of that market your business can realistically serve.
> 3. Which part you could plausibly win in the near term.
>
> These are often called TAM, SAM, and SOM.

The production explanation should be delivered in Persian.

# 14. Primary Evidence Classes

Phase 2 must distinguish between different evidence classes.

Use:

```text
PRIMARY
FIRST_PARTY
SECONDARY
DERIVED
ANECDOTAL
ASSUMED
UNKNOWN
```

---

# 15. PRIMARY Evidence

Examples:

- Official government statistics
- Official regulator data
- Official company filings
- Official price lists
- Official product pages
- Official industry association reports
- Original published research
- Direct customer interviews conducted for the project
- Direct surveys conducted for the project

Primary evidence is generally preferred when relevant and current.

---

# 16. FIRST_PARTY Evidence

Business-owned evidence includes:

- CRM data
- Sales history
- Customer support logs
- Website analytics
- Search queries
- Purchase history
- Returns
- Reviews collected by the business
- Customer interviews
- Sales-team notes
- Existing campaign data

For an active business, first-party data can be more strategically useful than generic market articles.

---

# 17. SECONDARY Evidence

Examples:

- Credible research firms
- Academic summaries
- Trade publications
- Established business publications
- Marketplaces
- Reputable comparison sites
- Distributor data
- Industry journalism

Secondary evidence must be evaluated for:

- date
- methodology
- geography
- relevance
- commercial bias

---

# 18. DERIVED Evidence

Derived evidence is a conclusion calculated or inferred from other evidence.

Example:

```yaml
finding:
  "Premium competitors cluster around a higher price band"

type:
  derived

based_on:
  - C012
  - C018
  - C022
  - C025
```

Derived findings must retain traceability.

---

# 19. ANECDOTAL Evidence

Examples:

- A small number of social comments
- Individual reviews
- Founder stories
- One customer complaint
- One salesperson's opinion

Anecdotal evidence can reveal useful hypotheses.

It must not be generalized automatically.

---

# 20. ASSUMED and UNKNOWN

If there is insufficient evidence:

```text
ASSUMED
```

means a plausible but unvalidated belief.

```text
UNKNOWN
```

means the system cannot responsibly conclude.

Both are acceptable research outcomes.

---

# 21. Evidence Status

Every important research statement should receive:

```text
SUPPORTED
PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED
CONTRADICTED
INCONCLUSIVE
NOT_RESEARCHED
```

---

# 22. Evidence Strength

Use an evidence strength scale:

```text
A — Strong
B — Good
C — Moderate
D — Weak
E — Speculative
```

The rating should consider:

- source authority
- source independence
- sample size
- recency
- geographic relevance
- category relevance
- methodological quality
- cross-source agreement

---

# 23. Confidence Is Not Evidence

Do not confuse model confidence with source quality.

Forbidden:

> I am 90% confident, therefore this is a fact.

Required logic:

```yaml
claim:
  "Customers strongly value same-day delivery"

evidence_strength:
  B

reason:
  "Repeated across first-party reviews, competitor reviews, and channel data"

confidence:
  medium_high
```

---

# 24. Source Traceability

Every important external claim must preserve:

```yaml
source_id:
title:
publisher:
url:
publication_date:
access_date:
geography:
source_type:
methodology_known:
relevance:
notes:
```

---

# 25. Citation Rule

The final customer-facing report should provide references for material research findings.

The report should avoid citation clutter for trivial statements.

Citations are especially important for:

- market size
- growth rate
- customer statistics
- legal/regulatory facts
- competitor pricing
- major trend claims
- channel statistics
- category behavior
- economic data

---

# 26. Freshness Rule

Research must be appropriate to the current date.

Time-sensitive information includes:

- prices
- competitor offers
- website messaging
- social-channel activity
- market size
- regulations
- platform behavior
- product availability
- consumer trends

The system must prefer recent evidence where the topic can change quickly.

Do not use old numbers as current facts merely because they rank highly in search results.

---

# 27. Date Labeling

When an important source is older, make the time context visible.

Example:

> This estimate is based on a 2024 report and should be treated as directional rather than a current 2026 market figure.

The final explanation must be in Persian.

---

# 28. Geography Discipline

Never use data from one geography as if it directly describes another geography.

Example:

US consumer behavior does not automatically equal Iranian consumer behavior.

If using international evidence as a proxy, label it:

```text
CROSS_MARKET_PROXY
```

and explain the limitation.

---

# 29. Category Discipline

Research the actual decision category, not merely the product keyword.

Example:

A coffee brand may compete with:

- other packaged coffee brands
- cafés
- instant coffee
- home brewing
- energy drinks
- tea in some usage occasions

The relevant category depends on the customer job and purchase situation.

---

# 30. Research Triangulation

Important claims should preferably be checked against more than one independent source or evidence type.

Example:

```text
Official market data
+
Competitor observation
+
Customer review patterns
```

Cross-source agreement increases confidence.

---

# 31. Independent Source Rule

Do not count ten pages that repeat the same original report as ten independent sources.

Track the likely original source when possible.

---

# 32. Research Depth Rule

Research depth should be proportional to decision impact.

Use:

```text
CRITICAL
HIGH
MEDIUM
LOW
```

Critical research questions deserve stronger validation.

Low-impact questions should not consume disproportionate effort.

---

# 33. Research Stop Rule

Do not research forever.

A topic may be marked sufficiently researched when:

- multiple credible sources agree
- the conclusion is stable enough for the next decision
- additional research is unlikely to materially change the decision
- remaining uncertainty is explicitly documented

---

# 34. Research Expansion Rule

Continue research when:

- high-quality sources conflict
- a critical claim relies on one weak source
- the geography does not match
- the evidence is outdated
- the customer and competitor evidence disagree
- the decision consequence is high
- the initial founder assumption remains ambiguous

---

# 35. Research Contradictions

Contradictory evidence must be preserved.

Do not hide disagreement.

Example:

```text
Source A:
Category demand is growing.

Source B:
Retail unit sales are declining.

Possible interpretation:
Revenue growth may be driven by price inflation rather than volume growth.
```

That interpretation must be labeled as an inference until verified.

---

# 36. Input Validation Gate

Before Phase 2 research begins, confirm that Phase 1 provides enough context.

At minimum:

- business description
- target geography
- category or product scope
- current business stage
- primary goal
- critical research questions

If one of these is missing, recover it from existing project information if possible.

Do not restart the whole interview.

# 40. Research Priority Logic

A question is usually `CRITICAL` when getting it wrong could invalidate:

- the market choice
- core product choice
- target customer direction
- pricing viability
- regulatory feasibility
- business model
- distribution feasibility

---

# 41. Example

Phase 1 assumption:

> Young consumers are probably our main audience.

Phase 2 research question:

> Which customer groups demonstrate the strongest need, purchase intent, current category participation, and reachable demand?

Do not research only age.

Research the underlying behavior.

---

# 42. Decision Link

Every critical research question should map to a later decision.

Example:

```yaml
question:
  "Which customer groups show the strongest potential?"

supports_phase_3_decisions:
  - target_market_selection
  - segment_priority
  - value_proposition
  - positioning
```

---

# 43. Research Method Selection

Possible methods include:

- Official data research
- Industry report research
- Competitor website audit
- Marketplace research
- Search behavior research
- Social listening
- Review mining
- Community/forum research
- Customer interview analysis
- Survey analysis
- First-party data analysis
- Price scraping / price observation
- Retail observation
- Content/message analysis
- Trend analysis

Use only methods appropriate to the question.

---

# 44. Research Method Limitation

Do not use a method simply because it is available.

Examples:

- Social comments are weak for estimating market size.
- Search volume does not equal sales.
- Competitor follower count does not equal market share.
- Survey intent does not equal actual purchasing behavior.
- A marketplace price does not automatically equal realized selling price.

---

# `Research Execution Plan`

```yaml
RQ001:

    - customer interviews if available
    - first-party sales data if available
  stop_condition:
    "Enough evidence to compare at least 3 meaningful segment candidates"
```

# STEP 1 — MARKET & CATEGORY INTELLIGENCE

# 47. Objective

Understand the market environment before deciding whom to target or how the brand should compete.

---

# 48. Define the Market Correctly

The system should define:

```text

SUBSTITUTES

GEOGRAPHIC MARKET
CUSTOMER TYPE
```

---

# 49. Category Definition

Ask or infer:

> What group of products/services does the customer compare this offer with when deciding what to do?

This may be broader or narrower than the business's internal product label.

---

# 50. Market Boundary Test

A useful market boundary should be:

- strategically meaningful
- measurable enough for research
- aligned with customer choice
- not so broad that findings become useless
- not so narrow that major alternatives disappear

---

# 51. Category Structure

Map relevant layers such as:

```text
CATEGORY
↓
SUBCATEGORY
↓
PRICE TIER
↓
USE CASE
↓
CHANNEL
↓
CUSTOMER TYPE
```

---

# 52. Market Maturity

Classify the market where useful:

```text
EMERGING
GROWING
MATURE
DECLINING
FRAGMENTED
CONSOLIDATING
UNCLEAR
```

This is an analytical classification, not a branding decision.

---

# 53. Market Drivers

Research factors that may drive demand, such as:

- demographics
- income
- technology
- lifestyle
- regulation
- convenience
- health
- culture
- infrastructure
- distribution
- economic conditions
- category innovation

---

# 54. Market Barriers

Research:

- regulation
- distribution barriers
- customer trust barriers
- switching costs
- price sensitivity
- supply constraints
- licensing
- infrastructure
- category education
- incumbent advantages

---

# 55. Market Size

Only estimate market size when it is decision-relevant and evidence exists.

Possible levels:

```text
TAM
SAM
SOM
```

But explain them in plain language.

---

# 56. Market Sizing Methods

Acceptable methods may include:

## Top-Down

Industry market total × relevant geographic/category share.

## Bottom-Up

Potential buyers × purchase frequency × average spend.

## Supply-Side

Estimated competitor / retail / transaction volumes.

Prefer triangulation where possible.

---

# 57. False Precision Rule

Do not produce a highly specific market-size number when evidence is weak.

Bad:

> The market is exactly 18.437 trillion IRR.

Better:

> Available evidence suggests a broad range, and the current estimate should be treated as directional.

---

# 58. Market Size Range

Where uncertainty is meaningful, use:

```text
LOW CASE
BASE CASE
HIGH CASE
```

and explain assumptions.

---

# 59. Growth

When reporting market growth, distinguish:

```text
Revenue Growth
Unit Growth
User Growth
Price Inflation
Nominal Growth
Real Growth
```

Do not treat nominal revenue growth as proof of demand growth.

---

# 60. Trend Intelligence

Research trends only if they matter to the business.

Separate:

```text
STRUCTURAL TREND

SEASONAL EFFECT

GLOBAL PROXY
```

# 62. Seasonality

Determine whether demand changes by:

- month
- season
- holidays
- events
- weekdays
- time of day
- academic calendar
- weather
- business cycles

Only include relevant factors.

---

# 63. Regulatory Research

When the category is regulated, research:

- licenses
- labeling
- advertising restrictions
- import/export rules
- health claims
- age limits
- taxes
- packaging rules
- data/privacy
- industry-specific restrictions

Legal/regulatory findings should be clearly distinguished from legal advice.

---

# 64. Market Economics

When relevant, examine:

- average selling prices
- gross margin norms
- cost structure
- distribution margin
- platform fees
- delivery costs
- major input cost volatility
- foreign-exchange exposure
- payment constraints

Do not invent unavailable benchmarks.

---

# 65. Market Opportunity Signals

Potential signals include:

- growing demand
- high dissatisfaction
- fragmented competition
- weak category education
- underserved geography
- poor customer experience
- narrow competitor positioning
- weak trust
- high search demand with weak supply
- inconvenient access
- unserved use case

Signals are not yet final strategic choices.

---

# 66. Market Threat Signals

Potential threats include:

- shrinking demand
- heavy regulation
- dominant incumbents
- extreme price competition
- low switching motivation
- commoditization
- low margins
- high customer acquisition difficulty
- supplier concentration
- unstable input costs

# STEP 2 — COMPETITORS, ALTERNATIVES & BENCHMARKING

# 69. Objective

Understand what the customer can choose instead, how current players compete, and where patterns or gaps exist.

---

# 70. Competitor Types

Separate:

```text
DIRECT
INDIRECT
SUBSTITUTE
STATUS_QUO
DO_NOTHING
INTERNAL_SOLUTION
```

---

# 71. Direct Competitor

A direct competitor serves a similar customer need with a similar category offer.

---

# 72. Indirect Competitor

An indirect competitor solves the same or similar customer problem through a different product or model.

---

# 73. Substitute

A substitute may satisfy the same usage occasion even if it belongs to another category.

---

# 74. Status Quo

Sometimes the strongest competitor is:

> doing nothing

or:

> continuing the existing habit.

This must be considered when relevant.

---

# 75. Competitor Discovery

Use multiple discovery routes:

- search engines
- maps/local business listings
- marketplaces
- category directories
- social platforms
- review sites
- industry reports
- customer mentions
- distributors
- retail listings
- first-party lost-deal information

---

# 76. Competitor Sample Design

Do not analyze only the most famous brands.

Aim for useful diversity such as:

- market leaders
- premium players
- low-price players
- digital-first players
- local players
- emerging challengers
- niche specialists

The exact sample depends on the market.

---

# 78. Messaging Analysis

Research what competitors repeatedly emphasize.

Examples:

- price
- quality
- speed
- expertise
- convenience
- status
- safety
- local origin
- technology
- customization
- simplicity
- trust

Do not infer actual brand ownership of a position merely from one headline.

Look for repetition across touchpoints.

---

# 79. Proof Analysis

Distinguish competitor claims from competitor proof.

Claim:

> Best quality.

Proof may include:

- certifications
- test results
- years in business
- guarantees
- customer count
- expert credentials
- transparent sourcing
- case studies
- ratings

---

# 80. Offer Architecture Benchmark

Compare:

- entry offer
- core offer
- premium offer
- bundles
- subscriptions
- guarantees
- trials
- free delivery thresholds
- loyalty
- add-ons
- upsells

---

# 81. Customer Experience Benchmark

Where observable, compare:

- search/discovery
- product education
- buying friction
- checkout
- delivery
- onboarding
- support
- returns
- repeat purchase
- loyalty mechanisms

---

# 82. Competitor Strength Rule

Do not label a competitor “strong” only because it has:

- many followers
- a modern website
- a nice logo
- high search ranking

Strength should be connected to meaningful evidence.

---

# 83. Competitor Weakness Rule

Do not invent weaknesses.

A weakness should be supported by evidence such as:

- repeated customer complaints
- poor coverage
- unclear offer
- limited availability
- price disadvantage
- poor service
- missing feature
- inconsistent experience

---

# 84. Review Mining

When enough public reviews exist, analyze:

```text
WHY PEOPLE BUY
WHAT THEY PRAISE
WHAT THEY COMPLAIN ABOUT
WHAT THEY EXPECTED
WHAT DISAPPOINTED THEM
WHAT THEY COMPARE AGAINST
WHAT WORDS THEY USE
WHAT OUTCOMES THEY VALUE
```

---

# 85. Review Sampling

Avoid cherry-picking.

Where possible, include:

- positive reviews
- neutral reviews
- negative reviews
- recent reviews
- multiple platforms
- multiple competitors

---

# 86. Review Pattern Threshold

Do not convert one complaint into a market-wide insight.

Use language such as:

```text
isolated
repeated
common
dominant
```

based on the observed pattern.

---

# 87. Competitor Position Map

Create comparative maps only using dimensions supported by evidence.

Example:

```text
LOW PRICE ←→ HIGH PRICE
GENERALIST ←→ SPECIALIST
CONVENIENCE ←→ EXPERTISE
MASS ←→ PREMIUM
```

Do not force arbitrary 2×2 maps when they do not clarify the market.

---

# 88. White Space Warning

A visual empty space on a competitor map does not automatically equal a profitable opportunity.

The space may be empty because:

- customers do not want it
- economics are poor
- regulation blocks it
- supply is difficult
- previous players failed

Mark white spaces as:

```text
OPPORTUNITY HYPOTHESIS
```

until demand evidence supports them.

---

# 90. Step 2 Completion Criteria

Step 2 is sufficiently complete when:

- direct competitors are mapped
- major indirect alternatives are mapped
- competitor types are separated
- offers and prices are compared
- message patterns are compared
- customer review themes are captured where possible
- possible gaps are evidence-labeled

# STEP 3 — CUSTOMER, DEMAND & BEHAVIOR INTELLIGENCE

# 91. Objective

Understand who experiences the problem, what they are trying to achieve, how they choose, what prevents purchase, and what evidence exists for demand.

---

# 92. Customer Research Principle

Do not begin by inventing demographic personas.

Start with:

- situations
- problems
- desired outcomes
- behavior
- purchase triggers
- barriers
- current alternatives

# 102. Customer Interview Mode

If direct interviews are available, the skill may create or analyze interviews.

- ask for examples

# 105. Survey Rule

# 174. Research Ethics

- fabricate interviews

# 175. Privacy

- minimize personal data
- avoid exposing private identifiers
- aggregate where possible
- use only information legitimately available to the project

# 176. No Fabricated Primary Research

If no interviews were conducted, never write:

> Customers told us...

Say:

> Public reviews suggest...

or:

> Available secondary evidence indicates...

Source type matters.

---

# 177. No Fake Statistics

Never create market percentages merely to make the report feel complete.

If a reliable percentage is unavailable:

> No reliable figure was found.

is a valid research result.

---

# 178. No Fake Market Share

Do not estimate competitor market share from:

- Instagram followers
- search rank
- review count

unless a transparent model and limitations are provided.

---

# 179. No “Best Competitor” Without Criteria

Do not say:

> Competitor X is the best.

Instead:

> Competitor X appears strongest on distribution coverage among the observed sample.

---

# 180. No Premature Customer Certainty

Do not say:

> Our target customer is women aged 25–34.

unless later strategy has selected them and evidence supports it.

Phase 2 may say:

> Women aged 25–34 appear in current customer evidence, but behavior-based segmentation remains more decision-relevant.

---

# 181. No Premature Positioning

Do not say:

> The brand should position itself as affordable luxury.

Phase 2 may say:

> There appears to be a possible gap between low-price mass offers and high-price premium offers; Phase 3 should test whether an accessible-premium direction is attractive and defensible.

---

# 182. No Premature Channel Strategy

Do not say:

> Focus only on Instagram.

Phase 2 may say:

> Instagram appears strong for discovery, while search and marketplaces show stronger purchase intent signals. Phase 3/marketing planning should define each channel's role.

---

# 183. No Premature Pricing Decision

Do not say:

> Price the product at 490,000.

unless Phase 2 specifically includes a validated pricing experiment and the result is strong enough for a recommendation.

Normally provide:

- market ranges
- customer price sensitivity evidence
- price tier implications
- test recommendations

---

# 184. Research Quality Gate

Before Phase 2 can be marked complete, check:

## Scope

- correct business
- correct geography
- correct category
- correct time window

## Evidence

- critical claims have sources
- weak evidence is labeled
- outdated evidence is caveated
- proxies are labeled
- conflicting evidence is visible

## Market

- category structure understood
- major barriers/drivers captured
- relevant market size/growth handled responsibly

## Competitors

- direct competitors mapped
- indirect alternatives considered
- pricing/offers compared
- customer feedback patterns assessed

## Customers

- segment candidates evidence-based
- needs/triggers/barriers identified
- demand signals separated by strength
- no fictional personas

## Commercial

- price context mapped
- channel roles mapped
- offer patterns understood

## Synthesis

- assumptions evaluated
- opportunities evidence-labeled
- risks documented
- unresolved questions preserved
- Phase 3 decision backlog created

Phase 2 can still complete with unresolved unknowns if they are not immediate blockers.

```text

```

and explain the needed validation.

# 189. Customer-Facing Writing Style

The Persian report should be:

- clear
- direct
- structured
- evidence-based
- non-academic
- beginner-friendly
- practical
- transparent about uncertainty

Avoid unnecessary English jargon.

If an English term is helpful, write Persian first.

Example:

> بخش‌بندی بازار (Market Segmentation)

not:

> Market Segmentation

# 190. Persian Number and Currency Rule

Use a consistent, readable format.

When reporting money:

- clearly state currency
- distinguish تومان from ریال
- never silently convert
- include date for time-sensitive prices when useful

# 191. Persian Citation Style

References may use numbered footnotes or a source list.

Each major source should include enough information to identify:

- title
- publisher
- date
- URL or source location
- access date when relevant

# 192. Report Accessibility

Whenever a concept is technical, include a short practical explanation.

Example:

# 193. Executive Recommendation Style

Recommendations must clearly indicate their status.

Use language equivalent to:

```text
STRATEGIC IMPLICATION
OPPORTUNITY HYPOTHESIS

UNRESOLVED QUESTION
```

Do not blur them together.

# 194. Research Failure Modes

## Failure 1 — Search Dump

### Symptom

The output is a long list of links and facts.

### Prevention

Synthesize evidence around decisions.

---

# 195. Failure 2 — Founder Confirmation Bias

### Symptom

The research tries only to prove what the founder already believes.

### Prevention

Actively search for disconfirming evidence.

---

# 196. Failure 3 — Competitor-Only Research

### Symptom

The system studies competitors but not customers.

### Prevention

Require customer/demand evidence.

---

# 197. Failure 4 — Customer-Only Research

### Symptom

The system studies complaints without understanding market economics or competition.

### Prevention

Combine customer, competitor, market, pricing, and channel evidence.

---

# 198. Failure 5 — Outdated Data

### Prevention

Check dates and label historical figures.

---

# 199. Failure 6 — Wrong Geography

### Prevention

Apply the geography discipline and proxy labels.

---

# 200. Failure 7 — Fake Precision

### Prevention

Use ranges, evidence grades, and uncertainty.

---

# 201. Failure 8 — Social Media = Market

### Prevention

Treat social data as one evidence type only.

---

# 202. Failure 9 — Followers = Competitor Strength

### Prevention

Evaluate commercially relevant signals.

---

# 203. Failure 10 — Average Competitor Price = Correct Price

### Prevention

Study price architecture, customer sensitivity, unit normalization, and value proof.

---

# 204. Failure 11 — Fictional Persona

### Prevention

Use evidence-based segment cards.

---

# 205. Failure 12 — White Space = Opportunity

### Prevention

Require demand evidence.

---

# 206. Failure 13 — Research Becomes Strategy

### Prevention

Maintain strict Phase 2 boundaries.

---

# 207. Failure 14 — Endless Research

### Prevention

Use stop conditions.

---

# 208. Failure 15 — Hidden Uncertainty

### Prevention

Maintain explicit limitations and unknowns.

---

# 209. Failure 16 — One Source Controls the Conclusion

### Prevention

Triangulate critical claims.

---

# 210. Failure 17 — Copying International Data Into Local Market

### Prevention

Use cross-market proxies only with explicit caveats.

---

# 211. Failure 18 — Researching Interesting but Irrelevant Topics

### Prevention

Link research questions to decisions.

# 212. Edge Case — No Reliable Market Size

Do not block the whole phase.

Use other evidence:

- transaction proxies
- customer volume estimates
- bottom-up scenarios
- competitor scale clues
- search demand
- channel volume
- first-party data

Then label the estimate appropriately.

---

# 213. Edge Case — No Public Competitors

Possible interpretations:

- category is very new
- market is local/offline
- customers use substitutes
- discovery method is wrong

Research alternatives and status quo.

---

# 214. Edge Case — No Customer Reviews

Use other evidence:

- interviews
- community discussions
- sales notes
- support logs
- search questions
- competitor FAQ
- retail observation
- surveys

---

# 215. Edge Case — Conflicting Customer Segments

Do not force a single answer.

Create multiple segment candidates and compare evidence.

---

# 216. Edge Case — Founder Disagrees With Evidence

Respectfully distinguish:

- founder experience
- external research
- first-party data

Do not automatically override either side.

Record the disagreement and identify what evidence would resolve it.

---

# 217. Edge Case — User Wants an Immediate Strategy

If critical research is incomplete, explain that a strategy can be drafted only as a hypothesis.

Do not present it as validated.

---

# 218. Edge Case — Research Reveals the Initial Idea Is Weak

Do not hide the result.

Explain:

- what evidence creates concern
- what could still make the idea viable
- what should be tested
- whether the business should narrow, change, or validate further

Do not force optimism.

---

# 219. Edge Case — Very Small Local Business

Do not over-engineer market sizing.

Focus on:

- local demand
- catchment
- competitors
- customer needs
- price
- reputation
- channels
- repeat purchase
- operational fit

---

# 220. Edge Case — Very New Category

Use:

- adjacent category evidence
- early adopter behavior
- problem evidence
- substitute behavior
- comparable markets
- direct validation tests

Label proxies carefully.

---

# 221. Edge Case — High-Regulation Market

Regulatory feasibility becomes a critical research gate.

Do not proceed as if marketing is the only issue.

---

# 222. Edge Case — Multiple Product Lines

Separate:

```text
BUSINESS-LEVEL MARKET
PRODUCT-LEVEL MARKET
```

Prioritize the product scope relevant to the current strategic project.

---

# 223. Edge Case — Multiple Countries

Research each priority country separately.

Only merge findings that genuinely generalize.

---

# 224. Edge Case — B2B With Few Buyers

Market research may rely more on:

- account lists
- procurement behavior
- expert interviews
- industry structure
- buyer concentration
- sales-cycle evidence

A small number of high-value buyers can still represent a meaningful market.

---

# 225. Edge Case — Existing Business Has Strong Data

Prefer actual business data over broad market stereotypes.

Example:

If public reports suggest one segment is dominant, but the company's profitable repeat customers come from another segment, preserve both facts and investigate why.

---

# 226. Continuous Research Principle

After Phase 2 completes, new evidence may appear.

The project should support:

```text
NEW_EVIDENCE
↓
UPDATE_CLAIM
↓
UPDATE_ASSUMPTION_STATUS
↓
UPDATE_STRATEGIC_IMPLICATION
```

Do not freeze research forever.

The report should include a version:

and research date.

Major market changes should trigger a meaningful version update.

# 230. Definition of Skill Success

This skill is successful when, at the end of Phase 2, a complete beginner:

1. Understands the market more clearly than before.
2. Understands who the meaningful competitors and alternatives are.
3. Understands the major customer groups without relying on fictional personas.
4. Understands the strongest customer problems and desired outcomes.
5. Understands the strongest purchase triggers and barriers.
6. Understands how customers compare options.
7. Understands the major price bands.
8. Understands how offers are structured in the market.
9. Understands which channels matter at different stages of the customer journey.
10. Knows which Phase 1 assumptions were supported.
11. Knows which assumptions were contradicted.
12. Knows which questions remain unresolved.
13. Understands the strongest market opportunities.
14. Understands the major business risks.
15. Can see the evidence behind important conclusions.
16. Is not forced to accept false certainty.
17. Has a clear Strategy Brief for Phase 3.
18. Is ready to make strategic brand and market choices based on evidence rather than guesswork.

# 231. Final Language Rule

The skill instructions, internal schemas, source ledgers, machine-readable state, research logic, and implementation details may be written in English.

However:

# The final `Market & Customer Intelligence Report` delivered to the customer MUST be in Persian.

The customer-facing report should:

- use natural Persian
- explain jargon
- use Persian headings
- distinguish evidence from inference
- distinguish findings from recommendations
- distinguish opportunities from confirmed decisions
- show important sources
- show limitations
- show contradictions
- show uncertainty
- be understandable without prior marketing knowledge
- be actionable enough to support Phase 3

---

# 232. Ultimate Phase 2 Rule

> **Phase 2 is not about deciding what the brand should say.**

Instead:

> **Phase 2 is about discovering what the market, customers, competitors, prices, channels, and evidence are actually telling us before we make strategic brand decisions.**

Operational interpretation:

> Start with the Phase 1 Research Brief. Research only questions that matter to decisions. Prefer strong and current evidence. Preserve source traceability. Challenge founder assumptions. Search for disconfirming evidence. Separate facts from inference. Do not invent market data, customer personas, market share, demand, or strategic certainty. Produce a Persian Market & Customer Intelligence Report and a Phase 3 Strategy Brief that make the next strategic decisions substantially safer and more informed.
