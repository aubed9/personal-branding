# 4. Skill Mission

The mission of this skill is to move the user from:

> “We know what the brand strategically means and why customers should choose it.”

to:

> “We know what kind of brand this must feel like, how it should behave, what personality it should consistently express, how it should relate to customers, how it should communicate, what emotional impression it should create, and what future naming, messaging, verbal identity, visual identity, and experience design must preserve.”

Phase 4 converts strategic meaning into identity logic.

---

```text
STRATEGY
```

It must not jump directly from strategy to decoration.

# 9. Strategy Before Identity

The identity must be derived from Phase 3.

Never begin with questions like:

> Do you like bold brands or minimal brands?

until strategic requirements are understood.

Personal taste is not the starting point.

---

# 10. Identity Is Not Decoration

Brand identity in Phase 4 means:

> the consistent human-like character, behavior, emotional expression, and communication posture through which the strategy becomes recognizable.

It is not merely:

- logo
- color
- font
- style

---

# 11. Character Is Not a Mascot

Brand character means the behavioral and expressive nature of the brand.

It does not mean creating a fictional mascot unless a later phase deliberately chooses one.

# STEP 0 — IDENTITY READINESS & STRATEGY TRANSLATION

# 21. Objective

Translate the Phase 3 strategic system into identity requirements before selecting personality traits.

---

# 22. Strategy Translation Questions

Convert strategy into identity questions such as:

- If the positioning is true, what kind of brand behavior makes it believable?
- What emotional impression supports the value proposition?
- What personality would strengthen the brand promise?
- What personality would weaken credibility?
- What communication posture matches the target customer's expectations?
- What should the identity avoid because competitors already overuse it?
- What experience signals can make the strategy visible?

---

# 23. Strategy-to-Identity Mapping

Create a map:

```text
STRATEGIC ELEMENT
↓
IDENTITY REQUIREMENT
```

Example:

```yaml
strategy:
  "Make expert-quality coffee simple for everyday consumers"

identity_requirement:
  - knowledgeable but not intimidating
  - clear rather than technical
  - confident but approachable
  - refined without elitism
```

---

# 24. Strategic Pillar Translation

Each strategic pillar should produce identity consequences.

Example:

```yaml
pillar:
  "Reliable simplicity"

identity_implications:
  - language should be clear
  - choices should feel manageable
  - tone should reduce anxiety
  - visual expression should avoid clutter
```

The final Persian report should explain these relationships.

---

# 25. Brand Promise Translation

Ask:

> If this promise were a person, how would they behave consistently?

This is a thinking tool, not literal anthropomorphism.

---

# 26. Customer Expectation Translation

Use customer evidence from Phase 2.

Example:

If customers are anxious and distrustful:

- identity may need calm confidence
- proof orientation
- transparency
- restraint

Not hype.

---

# 27. Category Convention Audit

Review common identity conventions in the category.

Examples:

- formal
- clinical
- luxurious
- youthful
- playful
- technical
- minimal
- loud
- traditional

The goal is not automatically to break conventions.

The goal is to know which conventions help comprehension and which create sameness.

---

# 28. Category Convention Types

Classify:

```text
USEFUL_EXPECTATION
NEUTRAL_CONVENTION
CROWDED_CLICHE
TRUST_SIGNAL
RISKY_CONVENTION
```

---

# 29. Identity Opportunity

Identify potential expressive gaps.

Example:

> Competitors communicate expertise through formality and jargon. There may be room for expertise expressed with clarity and accessibility.

This remains an identity opportunity until validated.

---

# 30. Identity Constraint Intake

Include:

- existing brand equity
- founder constraints
- cultural context
- language
- market norms
- regulatory limits
- team capabilities
- customer expectations
- brand architecture constraints

---

Create:

# `Identity Translation Brief`

Containing:

- strategy-to-identity requirements
- customer emotional needs
- category conventions
- identity opportunities
- identity risks
- required behaviors
- prohibited behaviors
- unresolved identity questions

Customer-facing version must be Persian.

# STEP 1 — BRAND CORE MEANING & IDENTITY TERRITORY

# 32. Objective

Define what the brand should mean emotionally and symbolically before defining its personality.

---

# 33. Core Meaning

Core meaning answers:

> Beyond the product itself, what should this brand consistently represent to the customer?

Examples:

- control
- progress
- reassurance
- possibility
- belonging
- confidence
- freedom
- mastery
- simplicity
- self-expression

Only select meanings supported by strategy.

---

# 34. Core Meaning Layers

Separate:

```text
FUNCTIONAL MEANING
EMOTIONAL MEANING
SOCIAL MEANING
SYMBOLIC MEANING
```

Not every brand requires all four.

---

# 35. Functional Meaning

What practical experience should the brand represent?

---

# 36. Emotional Meaning

What emotional state should interaction with the brand reinforce?

---

# 37. Social Meaning

What does choosing the brand communicate socially, if relevant?

---

# 38. Symbolic Meaning

What larger idea can the brand legitimately stand for?

Avoid grandiose symbolism unsupported by the business.

---

# 39. Identity Territory

Identity territory is:

> the emotional and expressive space within which the brand should consistently live.

Examples:

- calm mastery
- optimistic progress
- accessible expertise
- energetic confidence
- thoughtful simplicity
- warm precision

These are identity territories, not taglines.

---

# 40. Identity Territory Rule

A good territory should:

- reinforce positioning
- feel relevant to the customer
- fit the category
- be ownable enough
- support future verbal and visual work
- remain broad enough for long-term use
- remain specific enough to guide decisions

---

# 41. Territory Option Generation

Create 2–4 genuinely different identity territories when useful.

---

# 42. Identity Territory Card

```yaml
territory:
strategic_fit:
customer_fit:
emotional_effect:
category_fit:
distinctiveness:
verbal_implication:
visual_implication:
experience_implication:
risk:
```

---

# 43. Territory Selection Criteria

Evaluate:

```text
STRATEGIC_COHERENCE
CUSTOMER_RESONANCE
DISTINCTIVENESS
CREDIBILITY
FLEXIBILITY
LONGEVITY
CROSS_CHANNEL_USABILITY
EXECUTION_FEASIBILITY
```

---

# 44. Identity Territory Anti-Pattern

Avoid vague combinations such as:

> Modern, premium, innovative, friendly, minimal, bold, elegant.

This is not a coherent territory.

---

# 45. Emotional Outcome

Define the intended customer emotional response.

Example:

> “I feel capable and informed, not overwhelmed.”

This is stronger than:

> “The brand feels cool.”

---

# `Brand Core Meaning & Identity Territory`

- emotional meaning
- social/symbolic meaning if relevant
- selected identity territory

- risks
- rationale

# STEP 2 — BRAND PERSONALITY & CHARACTER SYSTEM

# 47. Objective

Define a coherent, prioritized personality that makes the strategy humanly recognizable.

---

# 48. Personality Definition

Brand personality is:

> the consistent set of human-like behavioral and expressive traits customers should perceive across interactions.

---

# 49. Character Definition

Brand character is broader than adjectives.

It includes:

- personality
- values in action
- behavior under pressure
- communication style
- relationship stance
- emotional posture

---

# 50. Trait Hierarchy

Separate:

```text
PRIMARY_TRAITS
SUPPORTING_TRAITS
PROHIBITED_TRAITS
```

---

# 51. Primary Traits

Prefer:

```text
3 to 4
```

primary traits.

They should drive actual decisions.

---

# 52. Supporting Traits

Use supporting traits only when they clarify nuance.

Avoid long adjective lists.

---

# 53. Prohibited Traits

Define traits the brand must not express.

Examples:

- arrogant
- childish
- cold
- vague
- aggressive
- elitist
- desperate
- overly technical
- sarcastic

Only include relevant risks.

---

# 54. Trait Structure

Every selected trait must include:

```yaml
trait:
meaning_for_this_brand:
why_it_matters:
observable_behavior:
communication_effect:
experience_effect:
what_it_is_not:
risk_if_overused:
```

---

# 55. Trait Specificity Rule

Avoid generic words without definition.

Bad:

> Friendly.

Better:

> Approachable — explains clearly, never talks down to the customer, and reduces unnecessary formality.

---

# 56. Trait Tension

Strong personalities often require controlled tension.

Examples:

```text
EXPERT + APPROACHABLE
BOLD + RESPONSIBLE
PREMIUM + HUMAN
ENERGETIC + CLEAR
WARM + PRECISE
SIMPLE + INTELLIGENT
```

---

# 57. Tension Rule

Use tension to avoid one-dimensional identity.

But ensure the traits are compatible.

---

# 58. Personality Slider Model

Where useful, define the brand on continuums such as:

```text
FORMAL ←→ INFORMAL
RESERVED ←→ EXPRESSIVE
CALM ←→ ENERGETIC
SERIOUS ←→ PLAYFUL
TECHNICAL ←→ SIMPLE
AUTHORITATIVE ←→ COLLABORATIVE
PREMIUM ←→ ACCESSIBLE
TRADITIONAL ←→ PROGRESSIVE
```

---

# 59. Slider Warning

Sliders are guidance, not scientific measurements.

Do not pretend a position such as “72% playful” is objectively measurable.

---

# 60. Archetype Use

Brand archetypes may be used only as optional inspiration.

They must not replace strategy.

---

# 61. Archetype Warning

Do not force every brand into one archetype.

Do not say:

> Your brand is the Hero, therefore it should behave this way.

Instead:

> Certain Hero-like behaviors may support the strategy, but the final character is defined by evidence and strategic requirements.

---

# 62. Archetype Output Boundary

If archetypes are used, label them:

```text
REFERENCE_ONLY
```

not the core identity system.

---

# 63. Personality Behavior Examples

For each primary trait, define:

```text
IN SALES
IN SUPPORT
IN SOCIAL MEDIA
IN ERROR / CRISIS
IN PRODUCT EDUCATION
IN INTERNAL DECISIONS
```

where relevant.

---

# 64. Character Under Pressure

A real identity must remain coherent when:

- customer is angry
- product fails
- delivery is delayed
- price rises
- criticism appears
- sales are weak

Define expected behavior.

---

# 65. Character Under Success

Also define how the brand behaves when:

- praised
- growing
- winning awards
- receiving high engagement

Avoid arrogance if inconsistent with strategy.

---

# 66. Personality Coherence Check

Check whether traits reinforce:

- target customer
- positioning
- value proposition
- brand promise
- strategic pillars

---

# 67. Personality Distinctiveness Check

Ask:

> If competitor names were removed, would this personality still sound like every brand in the category?

If yes, refine.

---

# 68. Step 2 Output

Create:

# `Brand Personality & Character System`

Including:

- primary traits
- supporting traits
- prohibited traits
- behavior per trait
- trait tensions
- slider guidance
- optional archetype reference
- behavior under pressure
- risks

# STEP 3 — BRAND RELATIONSHIP, ROLE & BEHAVIORAL PRINCIPLES

# 69. Objective

Define how the brand relates to the customer and how it should behave across real interactions.

---

# 70. Brand Relationship Role

Possible roles include:

```text
GUIDE
EXPERT
PARTNER
COACH
ENABLER
HOST
ALLY
CURATOR
CHALLENGER
PROTECTOR
FACILITATOR
```

Do not choose a role merely because it sounds attractive.

---

# 71. Relationship Role Question

Ask:

> What kind of relationship best helps the customer achieve the promised outcome?

---

# 72. Relationship Distance

Define the appropriate distance:

```text
CLOSE
WARM_PROFESSIONAL
PROFESSIONAL
FORMAL
INSTITUTIONAL
```

---

# 73. Authority Level

Define:

```text
LEADS
GUIDES
ADVISES
COLLABORATES
SERVES
```

---

# 74. Customer Agency

Decide whether communication should primarily:

- instruct
- recommend
- empower
- reassure
- challenge
- simplify
- co-create

---

# 75. Brand Role Anti-Pattern

Avoid:

> We are your best friend.

unless the category, customer, and strategy genuinely support that level of intimacy.

---

# 76. Behavioral Principles

Create:

```text
4 to 7
```

behavioral principles.

Each should guide actual behavior.

---

# 77. Behavioral Principle Structure

```yaml
principle:
meaning:
why:
customer_effect:
example_behavior:
anti_behavior:
```

---

# 78. Example Principle

```text
Explain before persuading.
```

Meaning:

> The brand earns trust by helping the customer understand the decision before trying to close the sale.

---

# 79. Transparency Behavior

If transparency is strategic, define what it means operationally.

Examples:

- clear pricing
- clear limitations
- clear comparison
- admitting uncertainty
- explaining trade-offs

---

# 80. Expertise Behavior

If expertise is strategic:

- teach clearly
- cite proof
- avoid unnecessary jargon
- distinguish fact from opinion
- admit limits

---

# 81. Warmth Behavior

If warmth is strategic:

- acknowledge customer context
- communicate respectfully
- avoid robotic phrasing
- remain useful

Warmth does not mean excessive informality.

---

# 82. Confidence Behavior

Confidence should be based on proof.

Avoid:

- hype
- unsupported superlatives
- aggressive certainty

---

# 83. Sales Behavior

Define how the brand sells.

Possible principles:

- advisory
- direct
- educational
- urgency-based
- proof-led
- consultative
- comparison-friendly

Use only what fits strategy.

---

# 84. Support Behavior

Define:

- response posture
- ownership
- apology behavior
- explanation
- recovery
- escalation

---

# 85. Error Behavior

When the brand makes a mistake:

```text
ACKNOWLEDGE
OWN
EXPLAIN
FIX
FOLLOW_UP
LEARN
```

Adapt to category.

---

# 86. Crisis Behavior

For relevant businesses, define communication principles under:

- outage
- safety issue
- public criticism
- data issue
- service failure
- controversial event

Do not create detailed crisis policy unless requested.

---

# 87. Internal Behavior

Brand identity should also guide internal decisions.

Example:

If the brand is “clear,” internal teams should avoid adding confusing complexity.

---

# 88. Step 3 Output

Create:

# `Brand Relationship & Behavior System`

Including:

- customer relationship role
- relationship distance
- authority style
- customer agency
- behavioral principles
- sales behavior
- support behavior
- mistake behavior
- crisis posture
- internal behavior implications

# STEP 4 — COMMUNICATION POSTURE & TONE BOUNDARIES

# 89. Objective

Define how the brand should communicate at a high level without yet writing the final tone-of-voice manual.

---

# 90. Communication Posture

Communication posture is:

> the consistent stance the brand takes when speaking with customers.

It includes:

- formality
- warmth
- directness
- confidence
- energy
- expertise
- simplicity
- emotionality
- humor
- authority

---

# 91. Tone vs Voice

Explain to the user:

```text
VOICE = relatively stable character
TONE = situational adjustment
```

Phase 4 defines voice direction and tone boundaries.

Phase 5 may create the detailed verbal identity system.

---

```text

CONVERSATIONAL

```

and explain why.

---

# 93. Warmth Level

Choose:

```text
LOW
MODERATE
HIGH
```

Define behavior, not just label.

---

# 94. Directness Level

Choose:

```text
SOFT
BALANCED
DIRECT
VERY_DIRECT
```

---

# 95. Confidence Level

Choose:

```text
CAUTIOUS
MEASURED
CONFIDENT
BOLD
```

Confidence must match proof.

---

# 96. Energy Level

Choose:

```text
CALM
BALANCED
ENERGETIC
HIGH_ENERGY
```

---

# 97. Expertise Expression

Choose:

```text
TECHNICAL
EXPERT_SIMPLIFIED
GUIDED
EVERYDAY
```

---

# 98. Humor Boundary

Choose:

```text
NONE
LIGHT
MODERATE
STRONG
```

with context restrictions.

---

# 99. Emotionality

Choose:

```text
RESTRAINED
BALANCED
EXPRESSIVE
```

---

# 100. Simplicity

Define whether the brand should:

- minimize jargon
- explain technical terms
- use short sentences
- use examples
- use metaphors
- preserve technical precision

---

# 101. Always / Sometimes / Never Model

Create:

```text
ALWAYS
SOMETIMES
NEVER
```

communication behavior.

Example:

```text
ALWAYS:
clear
respectful
useful

SOMETIMES:
playful
challenging

NEVER:
mocking
overclaiming
patronizing
```

---

# 102. Tone by Situation

Define high-level adaptations for:

- marketing
- sales
- support
- education
- crisis
- error
- celebration
- premium offer
- complaint

---

# 103. Tone Consistency Rule

Tone may change by situation.

Character must remain recognizable.

---

# 104. Customer Respect Rule

The brand must not communicate in a patronizing or belittling way.

Expertise should not make the customer feel unintelligent.

---

# 105. Hype Rule

If strategy requires trust or expertise, limit:

- exaggerated claims
- false urgency
- superlatives
- unsupported certainty

---

# 106. Humor Risk Rule

Humor must be constrained in:

- complaint resolution
- serious financial decisions
- health/safety contexts
- crisis
- legal/regulatory communication

where relevant.

---

# 107. Persian Language Considerations

For Persian-facing brands, define:

- formal vs conversational Persian
- use of colloquial expressions
- use of English words
- technical term handling
- honorific distance
- pronoun choice where relevant

Do not finalize detailed copy rules yet.

---

# 108. Bilingual / Multilingual Considerations

If multiple languages are required:

- preserve character across languages
- do not mechanically translate idioms
- define which identity traits must remain invariant
- allow cultural adaptation

---

# STEP 5 — IDENTITY PRINCIPLES, EXPERIENCE SIGNALS & CREATIVE GUARDRAILS

# 110. Objective

Convert character into practical guardrails for future verbal, visual, product, service, and experience work.

---

- relationship role shift

# 243. Definition of Skill Success

This skill succeeds when, at the end of Phase 4, a complete beginner:

1. Understands the core meaning of the brand.
2. Knows what emotional impression the brand should create.
3. Understands the selected identity territory.
4. Can describe the brand character in simple language.
5. Knows the 3–4 primary personality traits.
6. Knows what each trait means behaviorally.
7. Knows which traits are prohibited.
8. Understands the brand's relationship role with customers.
9. Understands how close, formal, authoritative, or collaborative the brand should feel.
10. Knows the behavioral principles.
11. Knows how the brand should behave when something goes wrong.
12. Understands the communication posture.
13. Understands the difference between voice and tone.
14. Knows the formality, warmth, directness, confidence, and energy boundaries.
15. Knows how humor should or should not be used.
16. Knows what the brand should always, sometimes, and never sound like.
17. Understands the identity principles.
18. Understands how verbal, visual, and experience work should interpret those principles.
19. Knows what the brand must feel like.
20. Knows what it must never feel like.
21. Understands what is stable and what may adapt by channel.
22. Knows which identity directions were rejected and why.
23. Understands the main identity risks.
24. Has a complete Phase 5 Verbal Identity Brief ready for execution.

# 244. Ultimate Phase 4 Rule

> **Phase 4 defines who the brand is before deciding exactly what the brand says or how the brand looks.**

Operational interpretation:

> Start from the locked Phase 3 strategy. Translate positioning, value proposition, brand promise, strategic pillars, customer expectations, and competitive context into identity requirements. Define the brand's core meaning, identity territory, personality, relationship role, behavioral principles, communication posture, tone boundaries, identity principles, experience signals, and creative guardrails. Preserve clear phase boundaries. Do not finalize naming, tagline, messaging system, logo, colors, typography, or visual design. Produce a Persian Brand Identity & Character Foundation and a structured Persian Phase 5 Verbal Identity Brief.
